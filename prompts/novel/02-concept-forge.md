# STAGE 0 — CONCEPT FORGE

Turns a premise, a fragment, a genre, or a single image into a **Story Design Document**.
This is the load-bearing stage. Everything downstream is execution.

> **Send:** `STAGE 0 — CONCEPT FORGE. Premise: [...]`
> If you have nothing: `STAGE 0 — CONCEPT FORGE. I have no premise. Interrogate me.`

---

## The premise test (run this first)

A premise is not a story. "A detective hunts a serial killer in Venice" is a setting and a
job description. A story needs an engine. Before designing anything, the model must confirm
the premise has all five:

1. **A character who wants something specific.** Not "redemption" — "to get her name back on
   the deed to the house."
2. **An obstacle that cannot be removed by talking.** A person, a system, a clock, a law of
   the world, or the character's own psychology.
3. **Stakes that escalate.** Failure must cost something the reader cares about, and the cost
   must grow.
4. **An answerable dramatic question.** "Will she X?" — with a yes or no available at the
   climax. If you can't write this sentence, there is no story yet.
5. **A reason it happens now.** Why does the story start on this day and not last year?

If any is missing, the model must say which, and propose three ways to add it. It may not
proceed on a premise that fails the test.

## The Story Design Document

```
STAGE 0 — CONCEPT FORGE.

Premise as given: [mine]

Work through the following in order. Interrogate me where my answer is thin — batch your
questions, max 5 per round. Do not proceed to Section 8 until Sections 1-7 are settled.
Where I have no preference, propose 3 options and recommend one with a reason.

1. THE ENGINE
   Dramatic question (one sentence, answerable yes/no at the climax).
   The promise of the premise — what the reader is buying and what they must get.
   Why now — the reason the story starts today.
   The central instability — the thing that cannot stay as it is.

2. PROTAGONIST
   Want (external, concrete, achievable or clearly not).
   Need (internal, usually opposed to the want).
   Ghost / wound — the past event that created the misbelief.
   Misbelief / the lie they live by — stated as a sentence they would agree with.
   Truth they must learn — stated as the direct contradiction of the misbelief.
   The gap — how far want and need are from each other at the start. (A big gap is where
   the book lives. A small gap means no arc.)
   Agency rating 1-10: how much of the plot they cause versus how much happens to them.
   What they are willing to do that a reader might not forgive.

3. ANTAGONIST / OPPOSING FORCE
   Who or what it is. If a person: their want, their own misbelief, and their argument —
   the case they would make for being right, stated so persuasively that a reader could
   agree. An antagonist who is wrong for no reason is a plot device.
   Why they are better at this than the protagonist (they must be, at first).
   Their plan — what they are doing offstage while the protagonist is reacting.
   Whether they escalate: what they do when the protagonist first wins.
   If the opposing force is not a person (society, nature, self, system), name the human
   face of it — the character through which it acts.

4. STAKES LADDER
   Personal / public / philosophical. What is lost at each. Then the escalation: what the
   stakes are at 25%, 50%, 75%, and the climax. Stakes that do not rise are the most
   common cause of a sagging middle.
   The "so what" test: state what the reader loses if the protagonist fails, in a way that
   would make a stranger care.

5. THEME AS ARGUMENT
   Not a topic ("grief") — a debatable proposition ("grief is a debt you keep paying
   because you think you owe it"). The plot is the evidence that argues for or against it.
   Where the theme is tested hardest. Which character embodies the counter-argument.
   Rule: the theme is never stated by the narrator and almost never stated outright in
   dialogue. If a character says the theme, it must be wrong, or be argued against.

6. GENRE, SHELF, AND OBLIGATORY SCENES
   Category, genre, subgenre, expected length, audience.
   Controlling value of the genre (the value the story turns on — e.g. love story =
   love/indifference/hate; crime = justice/injustice; thriller = life/death; society =
   inclusion/exclusion).
   Obligatory scenes and conventions the genre REQUIRES. Use the library below. A missing
   obligatory scene reads as a broken book even if the prose is excellent.
   Where this book obeys the conventions and where it deliberately subverts one — name
   exactly one subversion. More than one and you lose the shelf.
   3 comp titles with the axis of comparison for each.

7. THE ENDING — DECIDE IT NOW
   The climax: the choice the protagonist makes, and what it costs.
   Whether the dramatic question resolves yes, no, or yes-but/no-and.
   The final image and what it echoes from the opening image.
   The change: who the protagonist is at the end versus the start, demonstrated in an
   action, not a feeling.
   If the ending is not yet knowable, give me the 3 endings the premise actually supports
   and recommend one. Then lock it. Everything before it is built backward from here.

8. CONCEPT STRESS TEST
   Argue against this book. The strongest case that it is derivative, that the premise
   cannot sustain its length, that the middle will collapse, that the ending is unearned.
   Steelman it — no hedging. Then tell me which criticism actually landed and what we
   change in response. Do not soften this to be agreeable.

9. ONE-PAGE SYNOPSIS
   Present tense, causally linked, spoilers included, 400-600 words. Every sentence must
   be caused by the previous one. If a sentence could be deleted without breaking the
   chain, the chain is broken.

10. THE SEED LIST
    5-10 images, lines, objects, places, or moments that have no structural purpose yet
    but that you want in the book. These become motifs. Log them; the pipeline will place
    them.
```

## Genre obligatory-scene library

The model consults this when filling Section 6. A genre is a contract with the reader;
these are the terms.

**Love story / romance.** Lovers meet (or re-meet) under friction · the hole in each life
is established · attraction plus a real reason it cannot happen · escalating intimacy
interrupted by the internal wound · **midpoint: the false victory or the first "I love
you"** · complications from outside and inside · **the dark moment / breakup at ~75%** ·
the grand gesture or grovel, which must be an act of *change* not of persuasion · HEA or
HFN.
**Non-negotiable:** the thing that separates them must be the internal wound, not a
misunderstanding a single honest conversation would fix. If the breakup could be solved by
talking, it is a false conflict and the reader will hate it. The relationship is the A-plot;
everything else is B.

**Crime / mystery.** The crime or its discovery · the investigator takes the case (and has
a reason to) · the world of the crime is exposed · progressive complications and clues ·
**red herrings that are fair** · the false solution · the reveal, which recontextualizes
earlier scenes · justice or its failure, with a cost.
**Fair play (mandatory):** the criminal appears early; every clue needed for the solution is
on the page before the reveal; no unexplained poisons, no unestablished twins, no
supernatural solution; the detective is not the criminal unless it was signaled; motivation
is personal, not arbitrary; the solution must be deducible by an attentive reader.

**Thriller.** The villain's plan is set in motion · the protagonist is pulled in, often
unwittingly · **the protagonist becomes the target** · the clock is established and stays
visible · investigation/flight with escalating setbacks · the ally who is compromised ·
**the false victory** · the twist that recontextualizes the threat · the confrontation, in
which the protagonist must sacrifice something to win.
**Non-negotiable:** the villain is smarter and better-resourced than the protagonist for
most of the book. The protagonist survives by cost, not by cleverness arriving on time.

**Horror.** The ordinary world and its flaw · the intrusion / violation of the natural
order · denial — the characters rationalize · the proof that the monster is real ·
escalation and attrition · the discovery of the monster's nature or origin · **all is lost,
the monster wins** · the final confrontation at a cost · survival that is not victory.
**Non-negotiable:** dread comes from withholding, not from description. The reader's
imagination outperforms yours. Show the effect, delay the reveal, and pay for the reveal.

**Fantasy / quest.** The ordinary world with the wrongness in it · the call and the refusal
· **the cost of magic or power is established early and never violated** · crossing the
threshold · the world's rules demonstrated through consequence · tests, allies, enemies ·
the approach · the ordeal · the reward, which carries a price · the road back with the
antagonist escalated · the sacrifice · the return, changed.
**Non-negotiable:** a map-able journey and a power system with limits. Power that solves
problems without cost removes tension permanently.

**Science fiction.** The *novum* — the one new thing · its first human consequence · the
extrapolation (if this is true, then what else is true) · the encounter · the system or
society built around the novum · the crisis in which the novum's implications can no
longer be contained · the choice about what it means for the species/protagonist.
**Non-negotiable:** one novum, explored deeply, beats five explored shallowly.

**Action / adventure.** The hook that pulls the protagonist out of ordinary life · the
refusal and the point of no return · escalating set pieces with rising personal cost ·
**the midpoint reveal that changes what the mission actually is** · the betrayal or loss ·
all is lost · the final set piece, which must use a skill or fact established earlier ·
resolution that acknowledges the cost.

**Literary / character-driven.** The situation and its pressure · the complication · the
slow accumulation of detail that changes meaning · the crisis of self-understanding ·
the choice, often small and irreversible · the consequence · the resonance rather than the
resolution. Conflict may be internal and the ending may be ambiguous — but something must
still *change*, and the reader must be able to say what.

**YA.** The protagonist is 15–18 and the story is about agency: an adult-imposed order that
the protagonist must act against or within. First-person or close-third is the norm. Voice
is the product — it must be distinct within three paragraphs. No adult rescues the
protagonist at the climax; competence is earned on page.

**Historical.** The period is not wallpaper — it constrains what characters can know, say,
travel, eat, and believe. Establish the constraint, then make the plot depend on it.
Anachronism in attitude is worse than anachronism in detail.

**Narrative nonfiction.** The thesis, the through-line argument, the cast, the narrative
arc built from real events, the sourcing standard, and the scene/summary ratio. Every scene
needs a documented basis. Flag `[VERIFY]` on anything you are not certain of — never
dramatize a fact you invented.

## Output

The model writes `story-design.md` with all ten sections, then stops.

It ends with: `Story Design Document complete. Locked items: [list]. Open items: [list].
Ready for STAGE 1 — STRUCTURE.`
