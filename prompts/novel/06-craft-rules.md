# CRAFT RULES

Reference law for Gates 3 and 4. The model consults this when drafting and when auditing.
These are not stylistic preferences; they are the mechanics of readable fiction.

---

## 1. Motivation–Reaction Units (MRU)

From Dwight Swain. The single highest-impact rule in this file. It is what makes prose feel
like a person experiencing events rather than a camera reporting them.

For every significant stimulus, the response arrives **in this order**:

1. **Stimulus** — something happens, external and concrete.
2. **Involuntary reaction** — the body responds before the mind. Flinch, breath catch,
   stomach drop, skin prickle, hands going cold. Not chosen, not thought about.
3. **Emotional registration** — the feeling arrives second. Often as an image or an urge,
   not a label.
4. **Rational thought** — the mind catches up and interprets.
5. **Deliberate action** — what they do about it.
6. **Speech** — what they say, which usually comes last and is usually not the truth.

> **Broken:** *She felt a wave of anger and told him to get out.*
> **Correct order:** *The glass stopped halfway to the sink. Cold ran up her wrists. Of
> course he'd said it here, in her mother's kitchen, with the door open. She set the glass
> down carefully, because she wanted to throw it. "Get out."*

Same event. The second one takes 20 more words and is worth all of them.

**When to expand and when to compress:** full MRU treatment for the 2–4 biggest moments per
chapter. Compress or skip for minor stimuli. Applying full MRU to everything produces
melodrama and sludge. The craft is knowing which moments deserve the slow motion.

**Never reorder.** Speech before reaction reads as false. Action before thought reads as
reflex — which is correct only when the character is trained or panicked.

## 2. Scene and Sequel

**Scene** = goal → conflict → disaster.
- The POV character enters wanting something specific and achievable-in-principle.
- Something opposes it. Opposition must have a want of its own.
- The scene ends *worse than it began* or *better with a cost*: yes-but, no, no-and.
  Never a clean win. A clean win ends tension, and tension is the product.

**Sequel** = reaction → dilemma → decision.
- The character processes the disaster (MRU territory).
- They face a choice where every option costs something.
- They decide, and the decision becomes the goal of the next scene.

This chain is what makes a plot feel caused rather than assembled. If your chapter is a
list of scenes with no sequels, the protagonist is a passenger. If it's all sequels, nothing
is happening.

## 3. Psychic distance (John Gardner)

Five levels, from farthest to nearest:

1. **Objective / camera** — *It was winter of 1853. A man stepped off a train.*
2. **Reported** — *Henry J. Worthington stepped off the train, unaware that he would die
   within the year.*
3. **Indirect** — *Henry stepped down, glad to be out of that damned car. Freezing, this
   close to the lake.*
4. **Close indirect** — *God, the cold. Lake-effect, and his coat was still in the trunk.*
5. **Direct interiority** — *Freezing. Freezing and I left the coat. Brilliant.*

**Pick a distance per scene and hold it.** Most commercial fiction sits at 3–4; literary at
4–5; thrillers at 3–4 with dives to 5 at impact moments.

The two common failures: unintentional drift (starting at 2 and ending at 5 within a
paragraph), and distance violations (a close-POV narrator describing their own face or
using a word the character wouldn't know).

## 4. POV discipline

- One head per scene. Switch only at scene breaks, and only if the Beat Map assigns it.
- **The Knowledge Boundary:** the POV character cannot know, guess correctly without
  evidence, remember, or reference anything they have not perceived or been told. This is
  the most-violated rule in generated fiction and it is checked against the Ledger's
  Knowledge Tracker every gate.
- No describing the POV character's own appearance except through feeling (a hand, a
  reflection they'd plausibly notice, clothes pulling).
- Other characters are rendered only through observable behavior plus the POV character's
  inference — and the inference may be wrong. Wrong inferences are a feature.
- **Free indirect style** is the workhorse: third person, past tense, but the diction and
  syntax bleed into the character's own. *"He'd been here before. Twice, and both times it
  had gone badly."* No "he thought." The thought simply is.

---

## 5. Dialogue

- **Every character wants something in every exchange**, and it is not the same thing the
  other character wants. Dialogue without competing wants is exposition in costume.
- **Subtext:** what is meant must exceed what is said. People avoid the point, talk past
  each other, answer a different question, use the wrong word. Direct statement of feeling
  is a last resort and reads as weak almost every time.
- **"As you know, Bob"** is banned. Characters do not tell each other things they both
  already know. If the reader needs it, find a reason the character needs to say it —
  a new person present, an argument, a confession, a boast, a correction.
- **Tags:** "said" and "asked" are invisible. Adverb-tagged dialogue ("he said angrily")
  means the dialogue failed. Prefer an action beat over a tag when attribution is clear.
- **One speaker per paragraph.** Attribution ambiguity is a reader-stopping error.
- **Vary length and shape per character.** A character who speaks in fragments should never
  suddenly deliver a well-formed paragraph, unless that is the point.
- **Interrupt, deflect, go silent.** Real conversation is full of unfinished sentences.
- **Read it aloud test:** if a line is a complete grammatical sentence with a subordinating
  clause and no contraction, it is probably not speech.

---

## 6. Exposition

- **Justify the tell.** Information is allowed when the character has a reason to think it
  *right now*: a decision depends on it, a memory was triggered, someone contradicted them,
  they're rehearsing, they're lying.
- **Arrive late.** Deliver information at the last possible moment, when the reader is
  already leaning in for it. Front-loading worldbuilding is the fastest way to lose a reader.
- **In conflict.** The best exposition is contested — one character corrects another, or two
  accounts disagree, and the reader assembles the truth.
- **Never in the first three pages** unless it is a hook. Setup is not a license to explain.
- **The iceberg:** the character knows ten times what they say. Let the reader feel the
  unsaid mass.
- Budget: no more than one substantial expository beat per scene.

---

## 7. Description

- **Two to three concrete specifics beat a paragraph of generality.** "A diner" is nothing.
  "A diner with a dead neon R and a sugar dispenser taped at the base" is a place.
- **Filter through the POV character's concern.** A soldier, a thief, and a grieving mother
  entering the same room notice three different rooms. Description that would be identical
  for any observer is wasted words.
- **Engage at least two non-visual senses per scene.** Smell and sound are the fastest route
  to memory and presence. Generated prose is almost all visual, which is why it feels flat.
- **Verb over adjective, noun over adjective+noun.** "The door hung crooked on one hinge"
  beats "the old, decrepit door."
- **No weather openings** unless the weather is a force in the plot.
- **Establish a location in the first two sentences of arrival**, then stop describing and
  let action carry. Re-describing a room every time a character enters is a common failure.

---

## 8. Pacing and rhythm

- **Sentence length is a speed control.** Long sentences slow and deepen. Short ones
  accelerate and hit. A single four-word sentence after three long ones lands like a gunshot.
- **Paragraph length does the same at a larger scale.** White space reads fast.
- **Compression for transitions, expansion for turns.** Time spent on the page should be
  proportional to importance, not to duration in the story.
- **Tension is created by withholding,** not by intensity. A threat that has not arrived is
  scarier than one that has. Delay the reveal; show the effect first.
- **Cut the first and last paragraph of every scene and see if it still works.** Usually the
  first is throat-clearing and the last is a summary of meaning. Both are almost always
  deletable.
- **Repetition kills pace.** If a beat repeats at the same intensity, the reader stops
  feeling it. Complications must escalate, not restate.

---

## 9. Information management

Three modes, and knowing which you're in at any moment is the whole craft of suspense:

| Mode | Reader knows | Character knows | Produces |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Mystery** | less | more (or nobody knows) | curiosity |
| **Suspense** | more | less | dread |
| **Surprise** | neither | neither | shock |

Suspense is the strongest and most underused in generated fiction. Telling the reader the
bomb is under the table produces ten pages of tension; the explosion produces ten seconds of
surprise. **Choose deliberately per scene, and don't accidentally collapse suspense into
mystery by withholding what the POV character already knows.**

**Dramatic irony** — the reader understands more than a character on the page — is free
tension. Track it in the Ledger.

---

## 10. What must never happen

- **The Idiot Plot** — a plot that only functions because characters fail to ask an obvious
  question or check an obvious thing.
- **Deus ex machina** — rescue by an unestablished force.
- **Sudden competence** — a character performing a skill they have not been shown to have.
- **The antagonist's IQ drop** — a smart villain making a dumb mistake so the hero can win.
- **Coincidence as resolution** — coincidence may complicate, never resolve.
- **Offstage climax** — the important confrontation happening elsewhere and being reported.
- **Unearned epiphany** — a character changing their core belief without sufficient pressure
  applied on the page.
- **The false conflict** — a problem that a single honest conversation would solve. Especially
  fatal in romance.
- **Named emotions** — "she felt devastated" where behavior would have done it.
- **Thematic narration** — the narrator or a character explaining what the scene means.
