# STAGE 1 — STRUCTURE

Chooses a narrative architecture, maps it to a word count, and converts it into a
**chapter-numbered beat map**. This file produces the most important artifact in the whole
project: the thing that tells every chapter what its job is.

> **Send:** `STAGE 1 — STRUCTURE. Use the Story Design Document.`

---

## Step 1 — Architecture selection

The model recommends **one primary architecture and one secondary**, from the library below,
with the reason and the trade-off. It must not default to three-act because three-act is
familiar.

### 1. Three-Act (25 / 50 / 25)
Setup → Confrontation → Resolution. Break points at 25% and 75%.
**Use for:** almost anything, especially a first novel. **Cost:** the second act is 50% of
the book and this structure says nothing about how to fill it. Pair with Save the Cat or
Story Grid to solve that.

### 2. Save the Cat! — 15 beats
The most precise commercial map available. Percentages below are of total length.

| Beat | At | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| Opening Image | 0–1% | The "before" picture. Sets tone, world, flaw. |
| Theme Stated | 5% | Someone says the theme, usually as a question or offhand remark. The protagonist disagrees or doesn't register it. |
| Setup | 1–10% | The six things that need fixing. Establish want, world, stakes, supporting cast. |
| Catalyst | 10% | The inciting incident. Life cannot continue as it was. |
| Debate | 10–20% | Refusal, fear, the question "can I even do this?" |
| Break into Two | 20% | The protagonist *chooses* to act. Point of no return. |
| B Story | 22% | The relationship or subplot that carries the theme. |
| Fun and Games | 20–50% | The promise of the premise. The reason anyone bought this book. |
| Midpoint | 50% | **False victory or false defeat.** Stakes raise, the clock starts, A and B stories cross. |
| Bad Guys Close In | 50–75% | External pressure tightens AND the internal team fractures. |
| All Is Lost | 75% | The whiff of death. Something or someone dies — literally or symbolically. |
| Dark Night of the Soul | 75–80% | The lowest point. The misbelief finally stops working. |
| Break into Three | 80% | The truth is found — usually delivered by the B story. |
| Finale | 80–99% | Storm the castle → high tower surprise → dig deep down → execute new plan. |
| Final Image | 99–100% | Mirror of the opening image, proving change. |

**Use for:** commercial fiction of every genre, thrillers, YA, romance-adjacent.
**Cost:** mechanical if applied by rote; the beats must be caused by character, not by the clock.

### 3. Hero's Journey — 12 stages
Ordinary World · Call · Refusal · Mentor · Crossing the Threshold · Tests/Allies/Enemies ·
Approach to the Inmost Cave · **Ordeal** · Reward · Road Back · **Resurrection** · Return
with the Elixir.
**Use for:** quest fantasy, adventure, any story about transformation through a journey.
**Cost:** front-loaded and slow to start; the "return" act often runs out of steam. Best
used as an overlay on three-act rather than alone.

### 4. Story Grid — Five Commandments
**Fractal:** these five apply at scene level, sequence level, act level, subplot level, and
global level. That is what makes it so useful.
1. **Inciting Incident** — causal (a choice) or coincidental (an event). Global stories need
   one of each eventually.
2. **Progressive Complications** — each raises stakes or shifts a value; they must not
   repeat at the same intensity. They turn at a Crisis.
3. **Crisis** — a **best bad choice** (two terrible options) or **irreconcilable goods**
   (two things the character cannot both have). This is the hardest beat to write and the
   one that separates real books from plotted ones.
4. **Climax** — the choice made manifest in action.
5. **Resolution** — the new state, and what it costs.

**Use for:** everything, and especially for checking whether individual scenes work. A scene
with no crisis is a scene with no turn.

### 5. Seven-Point
Hook · Plot Turn 1 · Pinch 1 · Midpoint · Pinch 2 · Plot Turn 2 · Resolution.
Design **backwards**: write the Resolution first, then the Hook as its opposite, then the
midpoint, then the pinches (antagonist pressure), then the plot turns (protagonist agency).
**Use for:** tight plotting, thrillers, mysteries, shorter novels. **Cost:** thin on
character interiority.

### 6. Fichtean Curve
Opens *in medias res*, then a rising series of crises with minimal setup, each one
briefly resolved and immediately replaced by a worse one, building to the climax, then a
short falling action.
**Use for:** thrillers, horror, action, anything with a clock. **Cost:** little room for
character depth or worldbuilding; exhausting if sustained past ~90k words.

### 7. Dan Harmon Story Circle — 8 steps
You (comfort zone) · Need · Go (cross the threshold) · Search · Find (get what you wanted)
· Take (pay the price) · Return · Change.
**Use for:** character-centric stories, episodic or braided structures, anything where the
arc matters more than the plot. Excellent for subplot design — give every major character
their own circle.

### 8. Kishōtenketsu — 4 movements
**Ki** (introduction) · **Shō** (development) · **Ten** (the twist — an unexpected
juxtaposition, *not* a conflict) · **Ketsu** (conclusion, reconciling the two).
**Use for:** literary, quiet, slice-of-life, contemplative, and any story where conflict is
the wrong engine. **Cost:** commercial readers conditioned on Western structure may find it
undramatic. Requires exceptional prose to carry.

### 9. Snowflake (fractal expansion)
Not a shape but a method: one sentence → one paragraph (five sentences: setup, three
disasters, resolution) → character sheets → one-page synopsis → four-page synopsis → scene
list → scene drafts. Each level is an expansion of the previous.
**Use for:** when you don't know the book yet. **This engine's Stage 0 → 1 → 2 sequence is a
snowflake variant.**

### 10. Braided / multi-POV
Two to four storylines running in parallel, each with its own value arc, intersecting on a
schedule. Rules: every POV character needs a distinct want, a distinct voice, and a distinct
dramatic question; POV blocks must end on a hook that makes the reader accept the switch;
lines must converge or comment on each other — parallel lines that never touch are just two
shorter books stapled together.
**Use for:** epic fantasy, sagas, ensemble literary. **Cost:** doubles every continuity
problem. Do not attempt more than four POV characters in a first draft.

### 11. Non-linear / reverse / epistolary / frame
Fragmented chronology, reverse chronology (Memento-style), documents and letters, a frame
narrator. **Use for:** when the *order of revelation* is the point — mystery, trauma
narratives, memory. **Cost:** the reader needs an anchor within the first ten pages or they
will bail. Every jump must earn its confusion.

### Genre-specific overlays (apply on top of a primary)
- **Romancing the Beat** (Gwen Hayes) for romance: Introductions → Falling → Dark Moment →
  Resolution, with the relationship as A-plot.
- **Knox / Van Dine fair-play** for mystery (see `02-concept-forge.md`).
- **Seven Deadly Plot Problems** check for any structure: is there a "idiot plot" (works only
  because characters are stupid), a "talking heads" act, an unearned twist, a deus ex
  machina, a sagging middle, a subplot that outstays, or a climax resolved offstage?

## Step 2 — The math

```
Target word count: W
Chapter length: C  (recommend 2,000-3,500 for commercial, 1,500-2,500 for YA,
                    3,000-5,000 for literary)
Chapter count: N = W / C
```

Then convert every beat percentage into a chapter number: `beat chapter = round(percent × N)`.
The model must show this arithmetic explicitly, not estimate.

**Worked example:** 90,000 words at 3,000/chapter = 30 chapters.
Catalyst at 10% = 9,000 words = end of ch. 3. Break into Two at 20% = ch. 6. Midpoint at
50% = ch. 15. All Is Lost at 75% = ch. 22–23. Break into Three at 80% = ch. 24. Finale
begins ch. 24, ends ch. 29. Final image ch. 30.

The model flags immediately if a beat lands in a chapter that cannot hold it (e.g. the
midpoint falling in the same chapter as a plot turn).

## Step 3 — The Beat Map

This is the output. One row per chapter. Every chapter must have a **job** — if a chapter
has no job, it does not exist.

```
STAGE 1 — STRUCTURE. Using the Story Design Document:

1. RECOMMEND one primary and one secondary architecture. For each: why it fits this story,
   what it costs, and what it cannot do. Then name the one architecture you considered and
   rejected, and why.

2. SHOW THE MATH: target words, chapter length, chapter count, and the beat-to-chapter
   conversion table.

3. PRODUCE THE BEAT MAP as a table, one row per chapter:

| Ch | % | Beat | POV | Job of this chapter (one line) | Value shift (from → to) |
  Enters | Exits | Plants | Pays off | Words |

   Beat = the structural beat this chapter serves (multiple allowed).
   Job = what this chapter does for the book that no other chapter does.
   Value shift = the controlling value moving from one polarity/charge to another.
     A chapter whose value does not shift is flagged [NO TURN] and must be merged or cut.
   Plants / Pays off = setup planted here, or setup paid off here (cite the chapter it
     came from).

4. TENSION CURVE: a 1-10 tension rating per chapter, as a list. Flag any plateau of 3+
   chapters at the same level, any peak before the midpoint that is higher than the
   midpoint itself, and any descent that isn't intentional.

5. SUBPLOT MAP: for each subplot — who owns it, its want, its dramatic question, which
   chapters it appears in, where it intersects the A-plot, and where it resolves. A subplot
   that does not intersect the A-plot at least twice and does not pressure the theme should
   be cut.

6. ACT DIVISIONS: chapter ranges per act/sequence, with the turning point of each and the
   question the act poses.

7. AUDIT: run the structural red-flags check —
   - Is the midpoint a genuine context shift, or just a bigger event?
   - Does the protagonist cause the climax, or does the climax happen to them?
   - Is the crisis a real best-bad-choice, or a fake dilemma with an obvious answer?
   - Does any complication get resolved by coincidence?
   - Are there two chapters doing the same job?
   - Does the final image echo the opening image?
   - Is there an obligatory genre scene missing from the map?
   - Does the B story actually carry the theme?
   Report only failures.

8. Write the result to beat-map.md. Then STOP and wait for my approval.
```

---

## After approval

The Beat Map becomes a knowledge file. For each chapter call, you paste **only the current
chapter's row plus the two before and two after** — that is enough context to write well
without burning the window.

If the story moves during drafting (it will), update the Beat Map and log the change:

> `Beat Map drift: chapter N's job changed from X to Y because Z. Downstream effect on
> chapters N+1 through M: [...]. Re-approval needed: yes/no.`

Never silently revise the map.
